## A City Starts Bouncing

Sony's "Balls" begins quietly. A steep San Francisco street sits in afternoon light. Then a few colored balls skip into view. A few become hundreds. Hundreds become a flood. Soon the city is full of red, yellow, blue, green, and pink spheres ricocheting off roofs, cars, drainpipes, and pavement.

There is no product demonstration in the usual sense. No presenter adjusts the contrast. No diagram explains the screen. No family gathers around a television and gasps at the picture. The product appears only at the end, beside four words: "Colour like no other."

Created by Fallon London and directed by Nicolai Fuglsig in 2005, the commercial used 250,000 real bouncy balls and captured its central spectacle practically. José González's gentle cover of "Heartbeats" plays over the chaos, making the scene feel less like a stunt than a private daydream that somehow escaped into public space.

That tonal choice is the first clue to why the ad endures. This is not color presented as intensity. It is color presented as abundance. Sony does not ask viewers to inspect a technical improvement. It gives them the joy of seeing an ordinary city become briefly impossible.

## Awe Without a Mountain

Psychologists Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt describe awe as an emotion triggered by two things: perceived vastness and a need for accommodation. Something feels awe-inspiring when it is larger than our usual frame of reference and forces the mind to revise its understanding of what is happening.

"Balls" creates both conditions with a child's object. One rubber ball is trivial. A quarter of a million of them is weather. The scale changes the category. You know what a bouncy ball does; you do not know what a city-sized torrent of them will do next. The familiar becomes wonderfully impossible to keep track of.

This is awe stripped of solemnity. Advertising often borrows grandeur from mountains, oceans, space, and orchestral music. Sony found vastness in play. The commercial expands perception without making the viewer feel small or reverent. Its wonder is accessible. Anyone who has dropped a rubber ball can understand the basic physics, which makes the impossible scale feel oddly personal.

The contrast matters too. San Francisco's architecture supplies order: rows of houses, parked cars, straight curbs, fixed facades. The balls ignore that order. They pour through the grid in unpredictable trajectories, each one obeying gravity while the mass appears almost alive. Joy comes from watching rigidity loosen without anything truly threatening happening.

Positive emotion often broadens attention. We stop monitoring only what is necessary and begin noticing relationships, patterns, and possibilities. "Balls" invites exactly that kind of looking. The eye cannot follow everything, so it wanders. A cluster strikes a wall. One ball leaps higher than the rest. Color appears where the viewer did not expect to find it. The ad rewards attention without dictating where attention must go.

## Why Reality Matters

The practical decision to use real balls is more than production trivia. It gives the commercial weight.

By 2005, viewers were already fluent in digitally impossible images. CGI could have produced a cleaner flood, perfect trajectories, and any color Fallon wanted. But perfection would have weakened the idea. The pleasure comes from seeing color encounter the physical world: balls bunch together, scatter, disappear around corners, strike surfaces badly, and bounce at different heights.

Those irregularities make the image believable. Light behaves consistently. Gravity remains in charge, even if it is having an unusually colorful day. The city does not look painted over; it looks visited by an event. We stop evaluating an effect and simply watch something happen.

Reality also makes the ad's extravagance visible. A digital swarm can be duplicated with a command. A physical swarm implies planning, closure, launch systems, cameras, cleanup, and a willingness to tolerate uncertainty. The labor does not appear on screen, but its trace does. The viewer senses that somebody cared enough about the image to make the difficult version.

This is useful for a television brand. Screens are often sold with numbers that quickly lose meaning outside a showroom: resolution, contrast ratios, refresh rates, color ranges. "Balls" translates technical ambition into production ambition. Sony behaves like a company obsessed with what an image can feel like. The craft of the ad becomes evidence for the craft of the display.

## Turning a Feature Into a Feeling

The strategic challenge was simple. Color is a product attribute, but attributes are comparative and abstract. Unless two televisions sit side by side, a claim about better color is hard to experience. Sony solved that problem by refusing to make the ad a laboratory test.

Instead, it built a memory around the attribute. After watching "Balls," color no longer means a menu setting. It means surprise, scale, music, sunlight, and motion. The campaign gives the feature an emotional vocabulary.

This is a form of sensory branding, but unusually disciplined. The commercial does not pile every sensation into the frame. Its music is restrained. The camera often waits rather than chases. The city remains recognizably gray and beige, allowing the colored balls to do the expressive work. Abundance needs contrast or it becomes noise.

The ad also understands that demonstration does not have to be literal to be honest. A BRAVIA television will not release toys into the street. But the product promise is still demonstrated: Sony wants color to feel vivid enough to alter the emotional character of what you see. The metaphor exaggerates the experience while remaining faithful to the benefit.

That is why the final product shot does not feel disconnected. The television arrives after the feeling has been established and quietly claims authorship of it. The screen is not the star of the film. It is the proposed doorway back into the state the film created.

## The Takeaway

Sony could have asserted that BRAVIA produced exceptional color. Instead, it treated wonder as evidence, making color abundant enough to become an event and trusting viewers to complete the argument.

The principle is not to spend more money or stage a larger stunt. Scale alone can feel empty. The useful question is what emotional experience is hiding inside the product feature. Here, better color feels like an ordinary afternoon suddenly overflowing with possibility.

The commercial also shows the value of restraint around spectacle. The image is enormous, but the tone is intimate. The balls are chaotic, but the camera is patient. The palette is exuberant, but the city gives it room. Every creative choice protects the viewer's sense of discovery.

Most technology advertising makes the future look sleek enough to leave fingerprints on. "Balls" made it playful, physical, and slightly out of control.

That is the joy at the center of the campaign: the world has not been replaced. It has simply become more vivid than we remembered it could be.
