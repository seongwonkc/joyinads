## An Ad That Explains Nothing

In 2007, Cadbury aired a television commercial consisting of the following: a gorilla, seated at a drum kit, playing Phil Collins's "In the Air Tonight." The gorilla plays it well. With feeling, even. The famous drum fill arrives — one of the most anticipated moments in rock history — and the gorilla commits absolutely.

Then a brief pack shot. Then nothing.

No narrative. No product claim. No explanation of what a gorilla has to do with chocolate. Not even a particularly strong argument that this was an advertisement rather than a fever dream.

It was voted the best British TV ad of all time. Sales of Cadbury Dairy Milk increased by 9%.

## Incongruity Resolution, and Its Absence

Most humor runs a two-stage process called incongruity resolution: something breaks your expectations, then the punchline explains the break in a way that makes sense looking back. You laugh at the resolution.

The Cadbury gorilla creates incongruity on every available axis. Gorillas don't play drums. "In the Air Tonight" is not an advertising song. None of this has any business inside a chocolate commercial. Your brain loads up its incongruity-detection machinery and waits for the punchline.

The punchline never comes. There is no resolution. That absence *is* the mechanism.

Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren call this a benign violation — something that breaks a norm without threatening anyone. The humor comes not from resolving the violation but from your brain clocking that it's harmless. The gorilla is a benign violation that flatly refuses to explain itself, so the incongruity stays live the whole ninety seconds. The brain keeps waiting. The gorilla keeps drumming. And the longer the absurdity holds without collapsing, the better it feels.

What tips this into delight instead of confusion is the gorilla's commitment. No wink. No nudge, no acknowledgment that anything unusual is occurring. The gorilla drums as though nothing on earth matters more. Total earnestness inside total absurdity — that's the joke, and it's a joke that gets funnier the longer nobody blinks.

## Low-Involvement Processing

Robert Heath's research on advertising effectiveness named a phenomenon called low-involvement processing: ads that don't look like they're trying to persuade you often work better than ads that do.

The moment viewers sense a sales pitch, they switch on what Friestad and Wright called persuasion knowledge — the mental defenses that size up a message and decide how much of it to resist. *I see what you're doing here.* The Cadbury gorilla doesn't trip that wire. It reads as entertainment that happens to end with a chocolate logo, so the defenses never fully come online.

With no resistance mobilized, the good feeling the ad produces attaches to the brand unopposed. The affect heuristic handles the rest: you felt something nice; Cadbury was in the room; now Cadbury is quietly filed alongside that feeling.

And the pack shot lands at exactly the right second. After ninety seconds of accumulated delight, your brain is wide open to whatever shows up last. The logo arrives into a moment of elevated positive affect and gets tagged to it. That's the whole commercial engine — built with almost no product messaging at all.

## What Cadbury Got Right

The gorilla proved that joy can be a brand identity all by itself, with no product claim propping it up. Cadbury never said their chocolate was joyful. They simply *were* joyful — inexplicably, absurdly, unforgettably — for ninety seconds, and trusted you to draw your own line to the purple wrapper.

That trust matters more than it sounds. An ad that makes no logical argument is betting the audience can take in something strange and come out the other side happy without a guide. Most advertising doesn't trust anyone that far.

Plenty of brands have chased the formula since 2007. Unexpected animal, beloved song, no product message. Almost nobody has matched it, because the formula was never the point — the commitment was. The gorilla works because it is entirely sincere about being ridiculous. Imitators often look faintly pleased with how quirky they are being, and that self-awareness kills the joke on contact.
